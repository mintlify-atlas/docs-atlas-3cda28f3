NASA's **Artemis program** is the United States' current initiative to return humans to the Moon using the **Orion capsule** and the **Space Launch System (SLS)**. Every Artemis mission ends with an ocean splashdown, making U.S. Navy recovery operations an essential and non-negotiable part of mission success. The two missions documented in Nexus Ciencia — Artemis I and the upcoming Artemis II — represent the validation and operational phases of this end-to-end recovery chain.

## The Orion Capsule

The **Orion capsule** is the NASA crewed spacecraft at the center of both Artemis missions. Key design facts from the Nexus Ciencia knowledge base:

- Orion is purpose-built for **ocean recovery** (splashdown / *amerizaje*) — the same fundamental architecture used by Apollo capsules in the 1960s and 1970s.
- Its heat shield withstands re-entry from lunar-return velocities, significantly hotter than re-entry from low Earth orbit.
- Orion is not reusable in the same mission-to-mission cadence as a commercial capsule; each flight's hardware is inspected and in some cases refurbished after recovery.
- The capsule's ocean landing is the final phase of every Artemis mission, requiring pre-positioned U.S. Navy assets in the target recovery zone before launch.

## U.S. Navy Support Roles

NASA does not operate aircraft carriers, amphibious ships, military dive teams, or naval helicopters. For every Artemis splashdown — crewed or uncrewed — the agency relies entirely on formal agreements with the **U.S. Navy**. The support structure documented in Nexus Ciencia includes three distinct asset categories:

### Ship Assets

The Navy deploys **amphibious ships** to the recovery zone. For Artemis I, this was **USS Portland** — an amphibious transport dock capable of hosting the capsule on its well deck, providing medical facilities, and supporting diver operations. The same class of vessel will support Artemis II.

### Diver Teams

**Navy combat divers** are the first humans to reach the Orion capsule after splashdown. Their responsibilities include:

1. Swimming to the capsule immediately after water entry
2. Attaching **inflatable flotation collars** to stabilize the capsule in open ocean swells
3. Checking astronaut health through the hatch before extraction begins
4. Guiding the capsule and crew through the extraction sequence

### Helicopter Assets

**MH-60S helicopters** launched from the recovery ship provide air cover, communications relay, and the actual lift for astronaut extraction. The MH-60S hoists crew members from the capsule (or from the water if needed) and transports them to the medical deck aboard the ship.
