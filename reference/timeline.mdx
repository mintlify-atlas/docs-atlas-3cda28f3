The relationship between NASA and the U.S. Navy spans nearly seven decades — from Cold War satellite competition and the first ocean-floor descent to recovering Orion capsules in the Pacific. This timeline assembles every major milestone documented across Nexus Ciencia's knowledge cards and built-in chronology section, giving you a single reference that stretches from Sputnik to Artemis II.

<Note>
  The **built-in timeline section** (`#cronologia`) of the Nexus Ciencia portal focuses specifically on the **2020–2026** window — the Artemis era. The fuller history presented here is drawn from the portal's detailed knowledge cards (section `#explicaciones`).
</Note>

## Full Chronological Timeline

<Steps>
  <Step title="1957 — Sputnik and Project Vanguard">
    The Soviet Union launches **Sputnik**, the world's first artificial satellite, on October 4, 1957, igniting the Space Race. The United States responds with the **U.S. Navy's Project Vanguard** — the first official American program designed to put a scientific satellite into orbit during the International Geophysical Year (1957–1958). Project Vanguard was managed and operated by the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL), establishing the Navy as the country's first serious space-launch institution.
  </Step>

  <Step title="1958 — NASA Founded; NRL Team Transfers to Goddard">
    President Dwight D. Eisenhower signs the **National Aeronautics and Space Act** in October 1958, creating NASA as a purely civilian agency — a deliberate choice to prevent the militarization of outer space. When NASA opened its doors, it directly inherited the scientists, engineers, and laboratory infrastructure of the Naval Research Laboratory. This NRL team became the founding core of **Goddard Space Flight Center** in Maryland, NASA's first major research installation.
  </Step>

  <Step title="1960 — USS Trieste Reaches Challenger Deep">
    On January 23, 1960, the U.S. Navy bathyscaphe **Trieste** makes history by descending to the deepest known point on Earth — the **Challenger Deep** in the Mariana Trench, approximately **11,000 meters** (nearly 11 km) below sea level. The engineering required to withstand those crushing pressures would directly inform materials science for both deep-sea and space vehicles.
  </Step>

  <Step title="1960s — Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo Splashdown Recoveries">
    Throughout the Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo programs, every U.S. crewed spacecraft returning from orbit or the Moon splashed down in the **Pacific or Atlantic Ocean**. Because NASA does not operate aircraft carriers or military dive teams, it signed formal agreements with the U.S. Navy for all recoveries. Navy ships, helicopters, and combat divers secured flotation collars to capsules, verified astronaut health, and hoisted crews aboard vessel medical decks — a partnership that continued unbroken across all three programs.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2001–Present — NEEMO Underwater Astronaut Training">
    NASA launches the **NEEMO program (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations)**, sending astronauts to live for weeks at a time aboard **Aquarius** — an underwater research habitat anchored 19 meters below the ocean surface off the coast of Florida. The near-weightless conditions of neutral buoyancy simulate the microgravity of spacewalks. Crews practice geological sampling tools intended for future lunar surface traverses, stress-test communications under isolation, and study team dynamics cut off from the rest of the world.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2022 — Artemis I: First Orion Splashdown Recovery">
    In December 2022, NASA's **Artemis I** mission sends an uncrewed Orion capsule on a lunar flyby and returns it to Earth. The U.S. Navy recovers the capsule from the **Pacific Ocean** using **USS Portland**, validating the full set of recovery procedures — diver deployment, flotation collar attachment, helicopter extraction, and shipboard processing — that will be used for crewed missions.
  </Step>

  <Step title="2026 — Artemis II: First Crewed Lunar Flyby of the Artemis Era">
    **Artemis II** is scheduled to carry **four astronauts** around the Moon aboard the Orion capsule. The mission follows a circumlunar trajectory and does **not** land on the Moon; its primary purpose is to validate life-support systems in deep space before lunar surface missions begin. At mission end, the Orion capsule will again splash down in the ocean and be recovered by U.S. Navy assets using the procedures proved during Artemis I.
  </Step>
</Steps>
