## Space & NASA Terms

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Artemis Program">
    NASA's current program to return humans to the Moon. The Artemis program uses the **Orion capsule** mounted on the **Space Launch System (SLS)** rocket. Artemis I (2022) was an uncrewed test flight recovered by the U.S. Navy in the Pacific Ocean. Artemis II (2026) will carry four astronauts on a circumlunar flyby — the first crewed lunar mission since Apollo 17. Artemis III will attempt the first lunar surface landing of the program.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Goddard Space Flight Center">
    NASA's first major research installation, located in Greenbelt, Maryland. Goddard was founded by the team of scientists and engineers who transferred from the **Naval Research Laboratory (NRL)** when NASA was created in 1958. The NRL personnel had been running **Project Vanguard**, and their expertise in rocketry, satellite systems, and space physics formed the technical foundation of the new civilian agency.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="NEEMO — NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations">
    An ongoing NASA training program (2001–present) in which astronaut crews live and work aboard the **Aquarius** underwater habitat, anchored 19 meters below the ocean surface off the coast of Florida. NEEMO exploits the **neutral buoyancy** of underwater environments to simulate microgravity, allowing crews to rehearse spacewalk procedures, test geological tools intended for lunar traverses, and practice communications and teamwork under conditions of total isolation.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Orion Capsule">
    NASA's crewed spacecraft used across the Artemis program. Orion is specifically designed for **ocean recovery (amerizaje / splashdown)** after returning from the Moon or deep-space trajectories. Its heat shield is rated for the higher re-entry velocities of lunar return — significantly more demanding than re-entry from low Earth orbit. After every mission, Orion splashes down in the Pacific Ocean and is recovered by U.S. Navy ships and diver teams.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Project Vanguard">
    The United States' first official satellite program, managed by the **Naval Research Laboratory (NRL)** from 1956 to 1959. Project Vanguard was the American response to the Soviet Sputnik launch, and was designed to place a scientific satellite into orbit during the International Geophysical Year (1957–1958). When NASA was created in 1958, the Vanguard team and NRL infrastructure were absorbed into the new agency, directly seeding **Goddard Space Flight Center**.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## U.S. Navy Terms

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Amerizaje / Splashdown">
    A water landing by a spacecraft returning from orbit or the Moon. In Spanish, *amerizaje* (literally "sea-landing") is the term used throughout Nexus Ciencia. In English, "splashdown" is the standard NASA term. Every U.S. crewed capsule — from Mercury through Apollo and now Orion — has used ocean splashdown as its primary landing mode, requiring pre-positioned U.S. Navy ships, divers, and helicopters to carry out the recovery.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="MH-60S Helicopter">
    The U.S. Navy helicopter variant used in astronaut recovery operations after splashdown. MH-60S aircraft are launched from the recovery ship and orbit the capsule's landing zone, providing communications relay and air support. Once Navy divers have attached flotation collars and verified crew health, the MH-60S performs the hoist extraction — lifting astronauts from the capsule or the water surface to the ship's medical deck.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Naval Research Laboratory (NRL)">
    The U.S. Navy's corporate research institution, founded in 1923. The NRL ran **Project Vanguard** — America's first satellite program — and employed the team of scientists and engineers who would go on to found **Goddard Space Flight Center** when NASA was established in 1958. The NRL's contributions mean that the Navy played a foundational, if indirect, role in the creation of NASA's technical infrastructure.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Trieste">
    The U.S. Navy bathyscaphe (a type of deep-diving submarine) that made history on January 23, 1960, by descending to **Challenger Deep** — the deepest known point on Earth, approximately 11,000 meters below the ocean surface in the Mariana Trench. The engineering solutions developed to withstand the crushing pressures at that depth informed the materials science behind both deep-sea exploration vehicles and spacecraft designed for extreme pressure differentials.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Ocean & Deep-Sea Terms

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Aquarius">
    An underwater research habitat anchored approximately 19 meters below the ocean surface off the coast of Florida. Aquarius is the base of operations for NASA's **NEEMO** missions, where astronaut crews live for weeks at a time to simulate the isolation, confined-space teamwork, and near-weightless conditions they will encounter during lunar surface operations and spacewalks.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Challenger Deep">
    The deepest known point on Earth, located in the **Mariana Trench** in the western Pacific Ocean, at approximately **11,000 meters** (nearly 11 km) below sea level. Challenger Deep was first reached by humans in 1960 by the U.S. Navy bathyscaphe **Trieste**. The extreme pressure, total darkness, and absence of sunlight at Challenger Deep make it an important natural laboratory for studying **extremophile** life forms and for developing the engineering tolerances used in both deep-sea vehicles and spacecraft.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Enceladus">
    One of Saturn's moons, and a high-priority target for NASA's astrobiology research. Enceladus is known to have **active hydrothermal vents** beneath its icy surface — a geological feature associated with life-sustaining chemistry. NASA studies the extremophile organisms that thrive around hydrothermal vents on Earth (including in the deep ocean) to design the instruments that will search for signs of life in Enceladus' subsurface ocean.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Europa">
    One of Jupiter's largest moons, and one of the most scientifically compelling candidates for extraterrestrial life in the solar system. Europa has a global subsurface ocean of liquid water beneath a frozen ice shell. NASA uses research from deep-ocean environments on Earth — particularly the biology of organisms that live without sunlight at extreme depth and pressure — to model how life might exist in Europa's ocean and to design life-detection instruments for future missions.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Extremophile">
    An organism that thrives in environmental conditions that would be lethal to most life on Earth — including total absence of sunlight, crushing hydrostatic pressure, extreme temperatures, and high acidity. Extremophiles were discovered in abundance at deep-ocean sites such as the **Mariana Trench** and around hydrothermal vents. NASA studies extremophiles as biological models for the types of life that might exist in the subsurface oceans of **Europa** (Jupiter) and **Enceladus** (Saturn), informing the design of life-detection instruments for future planetary missions.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Neutral Buoyancy">
    The state in which an object (or person) neither sinks nor floats but remains suspended at a fixed depth in water — a condition of near-weightlessness. NASA uses neutral buoyancy training in underwater facilities (most notably the **Aquarius** habitat used in NEEMO missions) to simulate the microgravity environment of a spacewalk. Astronauts practice tool use, suit mobility, and extravehicular activity procedures while neutrally buoyant, because the sensation closely approximates working in the vacuum of space.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
