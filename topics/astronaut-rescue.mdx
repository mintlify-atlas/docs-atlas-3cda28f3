When a NASA capsule re-enters Earth's atmosphere after a mission, its journey is not over at touchdown — because touchdown happens in the middle of the ocean. Every crewed American spacecraft, from the earliest Mercury capsules to today's Orion vehicle, ends its flight with a **splashdown**, and without the U.S. Navy, no one would be there to bring the crew home safely.

## Why the Ocean, and Why the Navy?

American spacecraft returning from orbit or from lunar trajectories descend under parachutes and land in either the Pacific or the Atlantic Ocean. This is called **amerizaje** — the Spanish word used across the scientific community — or **splashdown** in English. The open ocean offers the safest landing zone: no mountains, no cities, no power lines, and enough surface area to account for targeting imprecision during re-entry.

The problem is that NASA has no aircraft carriers. It has no combat divers, no fleet of recovery ships, and no military helicopter squadrons on standby. NASA is a civilian scientific agency, and ocean recovery is a military-grade operation. To bridge that gap, NASA maintains **formal agreements with the U.S. Navy** that designate naval assets and personnel for every crewed ocean recovery, from the pre-launch planning phase through the moment the astronauts step onto the ship's medical deck.

## Naval Assets Deployed for Every Recovery

The U.S. Navy's recovery operations rely on a coordinated combination of ships, aircraft, and specially trained personnel:

- **Amphibious assault ships** — large naval vessels with flight decks and full medical facilities, including the *USS Anchorage*, *USS San Diego*, and *USS Portland*, which have all served as primary recovery ships.
- **MH-60S Seahawk helicopters** — medium-lift military helicopters capable of operating from ship decks, used to hoist astronauts from the capsule to the ship.
- **Navy SEAL divers** — combat-trained underwater specialists who are the first humans to reach a capsule after splashdown, operating in open-ocean conditions regardless of sea state or weather.

## The Recovery Sequence: Step by Step

<Steps>
  <Step title="Capsule Splashdown">
    The Orion (or historically, Mercury or Apollo) capsule splashes into the designated recovery zone in the Pacific or Atlantic Ocean. The ship and helicopter crews are already positioned nearby, having tracked the re-entry trajectory.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Navy SEAL Divers Deploy">
    MH-60S helicopters lift off from the recovery ship and fly to the capsule's position. Navy SEAL divers jump or rappel into the water and swim to the bobbing capsule to begin the immediate post-splashdown assessment.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Flotation Collar Secured">
    The SEAL divers attach an **inflatable flotation collar** around the base of the capsule. This ring of buoyancy stabilizes the vehicle in the water, preventing it from riding waves at dangerous angles and making it easier to open the hatch safely.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Astronaut Health Check">
    Before the hatch is opened, the divers communicate with the crew inside to assess their condition. This initial health check helps the ship's medical team prepare for the specific needs of the astronauts, who may be disoriented after days or weeks of microgravity.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hatch Opening and Crew Extraction">
    The divers assist in opening the capsule hatch. Astronauts who are able to move independently exit the capsule; those who need assistance are supported by the dive team. Each crew member is placed in a rescue basket or harness.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Helicopter Hoist to the Ship">
    The MH-60S helicopter hoists each astronaut from the water surface up to the aircraft. The helicopter then flies directly to the recovery ship, landing on the flight deck where the ship's medical team is waiting.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Medical Evaluation on the Ship's Deck">
    Astronauts are transferred to the ship's medical facilities for immediate evaluation. After extended missions, re-adaptation to Earth's gravity can take time — the ship's doctors monitor vital signs, muscle function, and hydration before any further transport is arranged.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Historical and Modern Recoveries

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Mercury & Apollo Era" icon="satellite">
    The splashdown tradition began with the very first American human spaceflights. Mercury astronauts splashed down in the Atlantic and were recovered by Navy ships and helicopters in the early 1960s. Apollo missions returning from the Moon splashed into the Pacific — including the iconic recovery of Apollo 11's crew in July 1969 by the *USS Hornet*. The Navy conducted dozens of successful recoveries across both programs without a single loss at sea.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Modern Artemis Recoveries" icon="rocket">
    The Artemis program has revived the splashdown tradition for the 21st century. During **Artemis I in December 2022**, the *USS Portland* successfully recovered the uncrewed Orion capsule in the Pacific Ocean after its lunar flyby — demonstrating that the recovery system works for the new generation of deep-space vehicles. **Artemis II**, scheduled for 2026 and carrying four astronauts on the first crewed lunar flyby since Apollo 17, will use identical Navy recovery procedures for a crewed Orion capsule.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## An Enduring Partnership

The NASA–Navy recovery partnership has now spanned more than six decades and multiple generations of spacecraft. It is one of the most quietly essential collaborations in the history of human spaceflight — invisible to the public until the moment a capsule hits the water, and then utterly indispensable. Every astronaut who has splashed down and come home safely owes part of that safe return to the sailors, divers, and aviators who were waiting in the ocean to meet them.
